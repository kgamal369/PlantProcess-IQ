# Core product positioning: BI-class experience, not a BI product

One important product-design principle must be made explicit across Chapters 1–4:

PlantProcess IQ is **not a generic BI tool**, but its dashboarding, charting, authoring, relationship modelling, filtering and exploration experience must reach the professional flexibility, clarity and usability level that customers already expect from platforms such as Qlik, Tableau and Power BI.

The difference is not that PlantProcess IQ should provide a weaker dashboard experience.

The difference is **what the product understands, learns and produces behind that experience**.

A traditional BI platform mainly helps the user connect prepared data, model relationships, create calculations and display the result.

PlantProcess IQ must provide the same class of freedom for:

* Connecting and modelling customer data.
* Declaring relationships between entities.
* Creating pages and sheets.
* Creating and editing charts, tables, KPIs and filters.
* Building calculated fields, measures and expressions.
* Creating reusable definitions.
* Applying dynamic and associative filtering.
* Drilling down and drilling through.
* Creating bookmarks, saved views and selections.
* Comparing periods, lines, equipment and operating contexts.
* Designing responsive layouts.
* Exploring data visually without programming.

However, PlantProcess IQ must then go substantially beyond BI.

Its primary purpose is to build and continuously enrich a governed, evidence-grade model of the customer’s industrial plant.

## 1. The plant model is the product’s intelligence foundation

The product must learn the plant through validated customer data and customer-authored relationships covering entities such as:

* Sites and plants.
* Areas and production units.
* Equipment and inspection devices.
* Materials and material identifiers.
* Material aliases across different source systems.
* Material genealogy and tracking paths.
* Parent and child materials.
* Production routes.
* Route steps and process stages.
* Operations and operation sequences.
* Process executions.
* Process parameters.
* Operating parameters.
* Quality inspections and quality events.
* Defects and defect classifications.
* Equipment states.
* Downtime and production-impact events.
* Maintenance and operational events.
* Product specifications.
* Production rules and operating limits.
* Taxonomy and master data imported from the customer’s own systems.

These entities and their values must not be hardcoded for one industry.

The schema must provide generic structural concepts, while all customer-specific vocabulary, entity types, relationships, fields, routes, defects, parameters and business rules enter through:

1. Customer data import.
2. Customer-authored configuration.
3. Registry derivation from the customer’s validated model.

## 2. The authored relationship model must be permanent

When the customer’s engineer links:

* A material identifier from one database to another identifier in an inspection system.
* Equipment in a historian to a production unit in MES.
* A process execution to a material.
* A quality event to a downstream material.
* A parent material to its child products.
* A route step to its equipment.
* An upstream parameter to a downstream quality outcome.

these joins must not exist only inside one dashboard or one temporary query.

They become the plant’s permanent, versioned and queryable relationship model.

The relationship must be declared once, validated once and then used consistently by:

* Canonical projection.
* Page and widget queries.
* Associative filtering.
* Drill-down.
* Drill-through.
* Genealogy.
* Statistical analysis.
* Correlation.
* Feature engineering.
* Machine-learning training.
* Prediction.
* Practice learning.
* Remediation search.
* Value calculation.
* Assistant retrieval and tools.
* Evidence and provenance.

This permanent plant model is one of the most important differences between PlantProcess IQ and a normal BI platform.

## 3. BI-class dashboard and authoring capability

The product must not force the customer to depend on developers for normal analytical needs.

The user must be able to create, through the interface:

* Any number of analysis pages within the licence and capacity limits.
* Sheets and page sections.
* Charts.
* Tables.
* Pivot-style views.
* KPIs.
* Calculated labels.
* Filters.
* Date and time controls.
* Numeric ranges.
* Search filters.
* Hierarchies.
* Drill-down paths.
* Drill-through targets.
* Calculated columns.
* Measures.
* Expressions.
* Reusable dimensions and measures.
* Reusable filters.
* Bookmarks and saved selections.
* Reports.
* Alerts.
* Analysis definitions.
* Model definitions.
* Practice-learning definitions.

The interface should provide two authoring paths:

### Guided path

A no-code experience for plant engineers:

* Drag and drop.
* Live schema and field browser.
* Registry-driven fields.
* Visual relationship creation.
* Chart recommendation.
* Context-sensitive property inspector.
* Immediate preview.
* Clear validation.
* Human-readable refusal messages.

### Advanced path

A controlled low-code or SQL experience:

* Professional expression editor.
* SQL editor with autocomplete.
* Schema and column discovery.
* Safe-function whitelist.
* Query preview.
* Result-schema inspection.
* Execution estimate.
* Compiled-SQL view.
* Version comparison.
* Dependency impact.
* Controlled publication.

Both paths must produce the same class of governed, named and versioned definition.

## 4. Dynamic filtering and associative exploration

Filtering must not be limited to a fixed row of dropdowns.

Every customer has different fields and different questions.

Filters must therefore be authorable objects derived from the customer’s own registry.

The product must support:

* List filters.
* Dropdown filters.
* Search filters.
* Date and date-range filters.
* Numeric-range filters.
* Hierarchical filters.
* Relative-time filters.
* Top-N filters.
* Expression-based filters.
* Cross-widget selections.
* Page-level filters.
* Widget-level permanent scope.
* User-level saved selections.
* Bookmarks.
* Associative possible, excluded, alternative and selected states.

A selection on any relevant visual must propagate through the permanent relationship model so that all dependent charts, tables, KPIs, predictions and evidence views update consistently.

This experience should be comparable in clarity and flexibility to Qlik-class associative exploration while remaining grounded in the plant’s governed relationship model.

## 5. The intelligence engines use the plant model

The purpose of building the plant model is not only to display charts.

The same canonical entities, genealogy, relationships, process routes and validated data must feed the intelligence layers.

### Statistics and correlation

The engines analyse relationships between:

* Process parameters.
* Operating parameters.
* Material attributes.
* Equipment conditions.
* Quality outcomes.
* Downtime.
* Throughput.
* Yield.
* Energy.
* Production-impact events.
* Other customer-registered outcomes.

### Machine learning

The system builds governed feature sets from:

* Material history.
* Genealogy.
* Process-stage history.
* Equipment history.
* Parameter windows.
* Route context.
* Product and specification context.
* Quality history.
* Operating practices.

### Practice learning

The system learns from the customer’s own validated operating history:

* Which combinations of parameters and operations produced the strongest outcomes.
* Which practices coincided with maximum productivity without failure.
* Which practices preceded defects, downtime or operational failures.
* Which current operations are drifting away from the plant’s own demonstrated best practice.

### Prediction

The system identifies an active material, equipment condition or production context that resembles historically risky conditions and produces:

* A specific predicted outcome.
* A defined prediction horizon.
* Risk and uncertainty.
* The contributing drivers.
* The relevant stages and genealogy.
* The historical evidence.
* Comparable successful and unsuccessful cases.

### Remediation

The product searches the customer’s own history for comparable early conditions that later achieved a better outcome and identifies what was done differently in the remaining production stages.

It then provides a supported remediation candidate with:

* The proposed later-stage practice.
* Historical support count.
* Expected-effect range.
* Comparable evidence.
* Limitations.
* Human approval.

The product must never automatically control the plant.

## 6. The display layer presents intelligence, not only data

The dashboard experience must display both ordinary operational data and generated intelligence.

Authorable pages and widgets must be able to consume:

* Canonical plant data.
* Statistical results.
* Correlation findings.
* Model results.
* Predictions.
* Prediction drivers.
* Practice benchmarks.
* Practice drift.
* Remediation candidates.
* Suggestion decisions.
* Value impacts.
* Data-quality conditions.
* Readiness states.
* Evidence and provenance.

A prediction or finding must behave like a first-class analytical object:

* It can be filtered.
* It can be compared.
* It can be drilled into.
* It can be placed in a chart.
* It can be included in a table.
* It can open its evidence.
* It can be linked to material genealogy.
* It can be included in a report.
* It can be explained by the Assistant.
* It can be tracked to its eventual outcome.

## 7. Product differentiation

The target is therefore not:

“Build a smaller Power BI inside PlantProcess IQ.”

The target is:

“Deliver a BI-class analytical and authoring experience over a permanent, customer-authored plant model, then use that model to learn the plant’s process behaviour, discover evidence-backed relationships, predict downstream outcomes, identify historically supported remediation and measure what happened afterwards.”

The dashboard is the interaction and presentation layer.

The plant model, genealogy, evidence chain, statistical discipline, practice learning, prediction, remediation and feedback loop are the product.

## 8. Required design implication

Please review Chapters 1–4 again under this positioning.

For every dashboard, charting, filtering and authoring capability, ask:

* Is it as generic and flexible as a mature BI product?
* Can the customer create it without a code change?
* Is every customer-specific option registry-driven?
* Does it use the permanent plant relationship model?
* Can it consume both canonical data and generated intelligence?
* Does every result carry evidence and provenance?
* Does the user experience remain clear at enterprise scale?
* Is the complete backend, database and validation contract specified?

For every intelligence capability, ask:

* Which plant entities does it use?
* How are features assembled through genealogy and route context?
* Which model or method computes it?
* Where is the result stored?
* How is it displayed and filtered?
* How does the user inspect the evidence?
* How is the decision recorded?
* How is the eventual outcome captured?
* How does validated feedback contribute to future governed learning?

This principle must become a cross-chapter design rule, not only a marketing statement.
